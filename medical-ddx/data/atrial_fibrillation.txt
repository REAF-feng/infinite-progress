Atrial Fibrillation

Overview:
Atrial fibrillation (AFib) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, characterized by rapid, irregular atrial electrical activity resulting in ineffective atrial contraction and irregular ventricular response.

Pathophysiology:
- Multiple reentrant wavelets in atrial tissue
- Triggered activity from pulmonary veins
- Loss of organized atrial contraction
- Irregular ventricular filling
- Increased risk of thromboembolism
- Potential for tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy

Classification:
By Duration:
Paroxysmal AFib:
- Self-terminating within 7 days
- Usually <24 hours
- Recurrent episodes

Persistent AFib:
- Sustained >7 days
- Requires intervention for termination
- May be long-standing (>12 months)

Permanent AFib:
- Accepted as permanent
- No attempts at rhythm control
- Focus on rate control

By Clinical Context:
First Detected:
- First documented episode
- May have been present for unknown duration

Valvular AFib:
- Associated with rheumatic mitral valve disease
- Mechanical or bioprosthetic heart valves
- Mitral valve repair

Non-valvular AFib:
- AFib without valvular disease
- Most common type

Clinical Presentation:
Symptoms:
- Palpitations
- Fatigue and weakness
- Dyspnea on exertion
- Chest discomfort
- Dizziness or syncope
- Reduced exercise tolerance
- May be asymptomatic

Physical Examination:
- Irregularly irregular pulse
- Variable intensity S1
- Signs of heart failure (if present)
- Signs of hyperthyroidism
- Pulse deficit (apical rate > radial rate)

Hemodynamic Effects:
- Loss of atrial kick (20-25% of cardiac output)
- Irregular ventricular filling
- Potential for rapid ventricular response
- Reduced cardiac output

Risk Factors:
Non-modifiable:
- Age (most important)
- Male gender
- Genetic factors
- Ethnicity (higher in Caucasians)

Cardiovascular:
- Hypertension
- Heart failure
- Coronary artery disease
- Valvular heart disease
- Cardiomyopathy
- Congenital heart disease

Other Medical Conditions:
- Hyperthyroidism
- Diabetes mellitus
- Chronic kidney disease
- Obesity
- Sleep apnea
- Chronic lung disease

Lifestyle Factors:
- Alcohol consumption
- Caffeine (controversial)
- Physical inactivity
- Smoking

Acute Precipitants:
- Acute illness
- Surgery
- Electrolyte imbalances
- Hypoxia
- Medication effects

Diagnosis:
Electrocardiogram:
- Absence of P waves
- Irregular R-R intervals
- Fibrillatory waves (f waves)
- Variable ventricular rate

Continuous Monitoring:
- Holter monitor (24-48 hours)
- Event monitors
- Implantable loop recorders
- Mobile cardiac telemetry

Laboratory Tests:
- Thyroid function tests
- Complete blood count
- Comprehensive metabolic panel
- BNP or NT-proBNP
- Magnesium and phosphorus

Imaging:
Echocardiography:
- Left ventricular function
- Left atrial size
- Valvular function
- Rule out structural heart disease

Additional Tests:
- Chest X-ray
- Exercise stress test
- Cardiac catheterization (if CAD suspected)

Risk Stratification:
CHA2DS2-VASc Score (Stroke Risk):
C - Congestive heart failure (1 point)
H - Hypertension (1 point)
A2 - Age ≥75 years (2 points)
D - Diabetes mellitus (1 point)
S2 - Stroke/TIA/thromboembolism history (2 points)
V - Vascular disease (1 point)
A - Age 65-74 years (1 point)
Sc - Sex category (female) (1 point)

Score interpretation:
- 0 (males) or 1 (females): Low risk
- 1 (males) or 2 (females): Consider anticoagulation
- ≥2 (males) or ≥3 (females): Anticoagulation recommended

HAS-BLED Score (Bleeding Risk):
H - Hypertension
A - Abnormal kidney/liver function
S - Stroke
B - Bleeding predisposition
L - Labile INR
E - Elderly (>65 years)
D - Drugs/alcohol

Treatment:
Rate Control:
Beta-blockers:
- Metoprolol, atenolol, propranolol
- First-line for rate control
- Target resting HR 60-100 bpm

Calcium Channel Blockers:
- Diltiazem, verapamil
- Alternative to beta-blockers
- Avoid in heart failure with reduced EF

Digoxin:
- For patients with heart failure
- Less effective during exercise
- Narrow therapeutic window

Rhythm Control:
Antiarrhythmic Drugs:
Class IA: Procainamide, disopyramide
Class IC: Flecainide, propafenone (no structural heart disease)
Class III: Amiodarone, dronedarone, sotalol, dofetilide

Drug Selection:
- Structural heart disease status
- Kidney/liver function
- Other medical conditions
- Drug interactions

Cardioversion:
Electrical Cardioversion:
- Synchronized DC shock
- More effective than pharmacologic
- Requires anticoagulation

Pharmacologic Cardioversion:
- IV or oral antiarrhythmic drugs
- Flecainide, propafenone, amiodarone
- Less effective than electrical

Catheter Ablation:
Pulmonary Vein Isolation:
- First-line for symptomatic paroxysmal AFib
- Superior to antiarrhythmic drugs
- Lower recurrence rates

Indications:
- Symptomatic AFib despite optimal medical therapy
- Patient preference over long-term drug therapy
- Drug intolerance or contraindications

Anticoagulation:
Warfarin:
- Vitamin K antagonist
- Target INR 2.0-3.0
- Requires regular monitoring

Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs):
- Dabigatran (thrombin inhibitor)
- Rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban (Factor Xa inhibitors)
- No routine monitoring required
- Fewer drug-food interactions

Anticoagulation Duration:
- Long-term for most patients with AFib
- Risk-benefit assessment
- Not routinely discontinued

Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion:
- Watchman device
- For patients with contraindications to anticoagulation
- Reduces stroke risk

Complications:
Thromboembolism:
- Stroke (most serious)
- Systemic embolism
- Pulmonary embolism

Hemodynamic:
- Heart failure
- Cardiomyopathy
- Hypotension

Treatment-related:
- Bleeding from anticoagulation
- Proarrhythmia from antiarrhythmic drugs
- Procedure-related complications

Management Strategies:
Rate vs Rhythm Control:
- No mortality difference in most patients
- Rhythm control for younger, symptomatic patients
- Rate control acceptable for older, asymptomatic patients

Integrated Care:
- Anticoagulation decision independent of rate/rhythm strategy
- Lifestyle modifications
- Treatment of underlying conditions
- Regular follow-up

Special Populations:
Acute Setting:
- Hemodynamically unstable: immediate cardioversion
- Stable patients: rate control, anticoagulation
- Duration >48 hours: TEE or 3 weeks anticoagulation before cardioversion

Heart Failure:
- Beta-blockers preferred for rate control
- Consider rhythm control
- Optimize heart failure medications

Perioperative:
- Continue anticoagulation if possible
- Bridge with heparin if high stroke risk
- Manage rate during surgery

Prognosis:
- Increased mortality risk (1.5-2x)
- Stroke risk varies with CHA2DS2-VASc score
- Quality of life impairment
- Progressive condition in many patients
- Better outcomes with early intervention

Prevention:
- Blood pressure control
- Weight management
- Alcohol moderation
- Treatment of sleep apnea
- Regular exercise
- Management of diabetes

Key Points:
- Most common sustained arrhythmia
- Stroke prevention is primary goal
- CHA2DS2-VASc score guides anticoagulation
- Rate vs rhythm control based on symptoms and age
- Catheter ablation increasingly used
- Multidisciplinary approach improves outcomes
- Patient education essential for management
