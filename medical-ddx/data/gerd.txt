Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

Overview:
GERD is a chronic digestive disorder characterized by the abnormal reflux of gastric contents into the esophagus, causing symptoms and/or complications. It occurs when the lower esophageal sphincter fails to function properly.

Pathophysiology:
- Lower esophageal sphincter (LES) dysfunction
- Impaired esophageal clearance
- Delayed gastric emptying
- Increased gastric acid production
- Hiatal hernia (often associated)
- Mucosal defense mechanisms failure

Mechanisms of Reflux:
Transient LES Relaxations:
- Most common mechanism
- Inappropriate LES relaxation
- Not triggered by swallowing
- Allows gastric contents to reflux

Mechanical Factors:
- Increased intra-abdominal pressure
- Anatomical abnormalities
- Hiatal hernia
- Pregnancy

Chemical Factors:
- Gastric acid
- Pepsin
- Bile acids
- Pancreatic enzymes

Clinical Presentation:
Typical Symptoms:
Heartburn:
- Burning sensation in chest
- Usually postprandial
- Worse when lying down
- Relieved by antacids

Regurgitation:
- Effortless return of gastric contents
- Sour or bitter taste
- May occur without warning
- Can lead to aspiration

Atypical Symptoms:
Respiratory:
- Chronic cough
- Asthma exacerbation
- Recurrent pneumonia
- Laryngitis and hoarseness

Cardiac:
- Non-cardiac chest pain
- May mimic angina
- Can be severe and prolonged

Oral/Dental:
- Dental erosions
- Halitosis
- Oral ulcers

Laryngeal:
- Chronic throat clearing
- Globus sensation
- Vocal cord irritation

Risk Factors:
Anatomical:
- Hiatal hernia
- Pregnancy
- Obesity
- Scleroderma

Lifestyle:
- Smoking
- Alcohol consumption
- Large meals
- Late evening meals
- Certain foods (spicy, acidic, fatty)
- Carbonated beverages

Medications:
- Calcium channel blockers
- Beta-blockers
- Nitrates
- Theophylline
- Anticholinergics
- NSAIDs

Diagnosis:
Clinical Diagnosis:
- Based on typical symptoms
- Response to acid suppression therapy
- No testing needed for uncomplicated cases

Empirical PPI Trial:
- 2-4 weeks of proton pump inhibitor
- Symptom improvement suggests GERD
- Cost-effective initial approach

Upper Endoscopy:
Indications:
- Alarm symptoms (dysphagia, weight loss, bleeding)
- Age >50 with new symptoms
- Failed PPI therapy
- Chronic symptoms (>5 years)

Findings:
- Erosive esophagitis (30% of patients)
- Barrett's esophagus
- Strictures
- Normal appearing mucosa (70% of patients)

Los Angeles Classification (Esophagitis):
- Grade A: Mucosal breaks <5mm
- Grade B: Mucosal breaks >5mm, not circumferential
- Grade C: Circumferential mucosal breaks
- Grade D: Extensive mucosal breaks (>75% circumference)

pH Monitoring:
24-hour pH Study:
- Gold standard for GERD diagnosis
- Measures acid exposure time
- Correlates symptoms with reflux events

Wireless pH Monitoring:
- 48-96 hour monitoring
- Better patient tolerance
- More comprehensive data

Impedance-pH Monitoring:
- Detects non-acid reflux
- Measures liquid and gas reflux
- Useful for PPI-refractory symptoms

Barium Swallow:
- Limited diagnostic value
- May show hiatal hernia
- Assess for structural abnormalities
- Not routinely recommended

Esophageal Manometry:
- Assesses LES pressure and function
- Evaluates esophageal motility
- Required before anti-reflux surgery

Differential Diagnosis:
- Functional dyspepsia
- Peptic ulcer disease
- Eosinophilic esophagitis
- Infectious esophagitis
- Coronary artery disease
- Esophageal motility disorders
- Biliary tract disease

Treatment:
Lifestyle Modifications:
Dietary Changes:
- Avoid trigger foods (chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, spicy foods)
- Smaller, more frequent meals
- Avoid eating 2-3 hours before bedtime
- Weight loss if overweight

Positional Changes:
- Elevate head of bed 6-8 inches
- Sleep on left side
- Avoid lying down after meals

Other Modifications:
- Smoking cessation
- Avoid tight clothing
- Medication review
- Stress reduction

Pharmacological Treatment:
Antacids:
- Calcium carbonate, aluminum hydroxide
- Rapid but short-term relief
- For occasional symptoms
- Can affect absorption of other medications

H2 Receptor Antagonists:
- Ranitidine, famotidine, cimetidine
- Moderate acid suppression
- Effective for mild-moderate symptoms
- Tolerance may develop

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs):
- Omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole
- Most effective acid suppression
- First-line therapy for erosive esophagitis
- Take 30-60 minutes before meals

PPI Dosing:
- Standard dose for 4-8 weeks initially
- Maintenance therapy often needed
- Step-down approach when possible
- Twice-daily dosing for severe disease

Prokinetic Agents:
- Metoclopramide (limited use due to side effects)
- Domperidone (not available in US)
- May help with gastroparesis-related GERD

Surgical Treatment:
Nissen Fundoplication:
- 360-degree wrap around esophagus
- Laparoscopic approach preferred
- Effective for acid and non-acid reflux
- Durable long-term results

Partial Fundoplication:
- Toupet (270-degree) or Dor (180-degree)
- Lower dysphagia rates
- May be less durable

Indications for Surgery:
- Failed medical therapy
- Young patients requiring long-term PPIs
- Large hiatal hernia with symptoms
- Extraesophageal symptoms
- Patient preference

LINX Device:
- Magnetic sphincter augmentation
- Preserves ability to belch and vomit
- Less invasive than fundoplication
- Limited long-term data

Endoscopic Therapies:
- Radiofrequency ablation (Stretta)
- Endoscopic plication
- Injection therapy
- Limited evidence for effectiveness

Complications:
Barrett's Esophagus:
- Intestinal metaplasia of esophageal epithelium
- Increased risk of adenocarcinoma
- Requires surveillance endoscopy
- More common in white males

Esophageal Stricture:
- Narrowing due to chronic inflammation
- Presents with dysphagia
- May require dilation
- Recurrence common

Aspiration Pneumonia:
- Due to regurgitation
- More common at night
- Can be life-threatening
- Prevention important

Dental Erosions:
- Chronic acid exposure
- Affects posterior teeth
- May be irreversible
- Preventive measures important

Special Populations:
Pregnancy:
- Common in pregnancy (45-85%)
- Related to hormonal changes and pressure
- Most medications contraindicated
- Lifestyle modifications primary treatment

Pediatric GERD:
- Often presents with different symptoms
- Failure to thrive, respiratory symptoms
- Conservative management preferred
- Surgery rarely indicated

Elderly:
- Atypical presentations common
- Higher complication rates
- Medication interactions important
- Careful monitoring needed

Prognosis:
- Chronic condition requiring long-term management
- Most patients respond well to treatment
- Lifestyle modifications provide benefit
- Complications preventable with proper treatment
- Quality of life significantly improved with therapy

Monitoring:
- Symptom assessment
- Medication adherence
- Surveillance for complications
- Endoscopy for Barrett's esophagus
- Consider step-down therapy when appropriate

Key Points:
- Common disorder affecting quality of life
- Clinical diagnosis in typical cases
- PPIs are most effective medical therapy
- Lifestyle modifications important adjunct
- Surgery effective for selected patients
- Complications require specialized management
- Long-term management usually required
- Patient education essential for success
