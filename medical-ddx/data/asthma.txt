Asthma: Comprehensive Clinical Management Guide

DEFINITION
Asthma is a heterogeneous disease characterized by chronic airway inflammation and variable expiratory airflow limitation. It is defined by a history of respiratory symptoms such as wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough that vary over time and in intensity, together with variable expiratory airflow limitation.

EPIDEMIOLOGY
- Affects approximately 300 million people worldwide
- 25 million Americans (7.7% of adults, 8.4% of children)
- Leading chronic disease in children
- More common in urban areas and developed countries
- Increasing prevalence globally
- Causes ~450,000 deaths annually worldwide
- Significant economic burden: >$80 billion annually in US

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Airway Inflammation:
- Chronic inflammatory process involving multiple cell types
- Eosinophils, neutrophils, T-lymphocytes, mast cells
- Release of inflammatory mediators
- Airway remodeling in severe/long-standing disease

Key Features:
1. Bronchial Hyperresponsiveness:
   - Exaggerated airway narrowing to stimuli
   - Smooth muscle contraction
   - Mucus hypersecretion

2. Variable Airflow Obstruction:
   - Reversible with bronchodilators
   - Circadian variation (worse at night/early morning)
   - Triggered by various stimuli

3. Airway Remodeling:
   - Subepithelial fibrosis
   - Smooth muscle hypertrophy
   - Mucus gland hyperplasia
   - May lead to fixed airflow obstruction

ASTHMA PHENOTYPES

Allergic Asthma:
- Most common in children
- Associated with other allergic diseases
- IgE-mediated
- Good response to inhaled corticosteroids

Non-Allergic Asthma:
- More common in adults
- Not associated with allergies
- Normal IgE levels
- May be more difficult to control

Exercise-Induced Asthma:
- Symptoms triggered by exercise
- More common in cold, dry air
- May be only manifestation

Occupational Asthma:
- Caused by workplace exposures
- Over 400 known sensitizing agents
- May persist after exposure cessation

Aspirin-Exacerbated Respiratory Disease (AERD):
- Asthma + nasal polyps + aspirin sensitivity
- Often severe and difficult to control
- Leukotriene pathway involvement

Severe Asthma:
- Requires high-intensity treatment
- Frequent exacerbations despite optimal therapy
- Subset may have fixed airflow obstruction

RISK FACTORS

Genetic Factors:
- Family history of asthma or allergies
- Multiple genes involved
- Gene-environment interactions

Environmental Factors:
1. Allergens:
   - House dust mites
   - Pet dander
   - Pollen
   - Mold/fungi
   - Cockroach allergen

2. Irritants:
   - Tobacco smoke
   - Air pollution
   - Strong odors/fumes
   - Weather changes

3. Infections:
   - Viral respiratory infections
   - RSV in infancy

4. Occupational Exposures:
   - Chemical sensitizers
   - Organic dusts
   - Metal salts

Host Factors:
- Obesity
- Female gender (adults)
- Premature birth
- Viral infections in infancy

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

Cardinal Symptoms:
1. Wheeze:
   - High-pitched musical sound
   - Usually expiratory
   - May be absent in severe obstruction

2. Shortness of Breath:
   - Variable severity
   - Often worse at night
   - Triggered by specific stimuli

3. Chest Tightness:
   - Sensation of constriction
   - May be only symptom

4. Cough:
   - Often non-productive
   - May be worse at night
   - Can be only symptom (cough-variant asthma)

Symptom Patterns:
- Variable over time
- Worse at night or early morning
- Triggered by exercise, emotions, laughter
- Associated with viral infections
- Seasonal patterns (allergic asthma)

Physical Examination:

During Stable Periods:
- May be completely normal
- Mild expiratory wheeze
- Prolonged expiratory phase

During Exacerbations:
- Audible wheeze
- Use of accessory muscles
- Supraclavicular retractions
- Pulsus paradoxus
- Cyanosis (severe)
- Inability to speak in full sentences

DIAGNOSIS

Clinical Assessment:
- Compatible symptoms
- Variable airflow limitation
- Response to bronchodilator treatment

Pulmonary Function Tests:

Spirometry:
- FEV1/FVC ratio typically normal or reduced
- Bronchodilator reversibility:
  * Increase in FEV1 ≥12% and ≥200mL from baseline
- May be normal between exacerbations

Peak Flow Monitoring:
- Useful for monitoring
- >20% variability suggestive of asthma
- Identify triggers and patterns

Bronchial Challenge Tests:
- Methacholine or exercise challenge
- Demonstrates airway hyperresponsiveness
- Used when spirometry normal

Additional Testing:

Allergy Testing:
- Skin prick tests or serum IgE
- Identify relevant allergens
- Guide avoidance measures

Inflammatory Markers:
- Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)
- Elevated in allergic asthma
- Predicts steroid responsiveness

Sputum Eosinophils:
- Research tool primarily
- Guide anti-inflammatory therapy

Chest X-ray:
- Usually normal
- Rule out complications or other diseases

ASTHMA CONTROL ASSESSMENT

Well-Controlled Asthma:
- Symptoms ≤2 days per week
- Night-time awakenings ≤2 per month
- Short-acting bronchodilator use ≤2 days per week
- No interference with normal activities
- Normal lung function

Not Well-Controlled:
- Symptoms >2 days per week
- Night-time awakenings 1-3 times per week
- SABA use >2 days per week
- Some limitation of normal activities
- FEV1 60-80% predicted

Very Poorly Controlled:
- Daily symptoms
- Nightly awakenings ≥4 times per week
- SABA use several times per day
- Extremely limited activities
- FEV1 <60% predicted

Asthma Control Test (ACT):
- 5-question validated tool
- Score ≥20 indicates good control
- Useful for monitoring

MANAGEMENT

Goals of Asthma Treatment:
1. Achieve and maintain symptom control
2. Maintain normal activity levels
3. Minimize future risk of exacerbations
4. Prevent development of fixed airflow limitation
5. Minimize side effects of treatment

Stepwise Approach:

Step 1 (Mild Intermittent):
- As-needed SABA alone
- Consider low-dose ICS-formoterol

Step 2 (Mild Persistent):
- Low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)
- Alternative: Leukotriene modifier

Step 3 (Moderate Persistent):
- Low-dose ICS + LABA or
- Medium-dose ICS

Step 4 (Moderate-Severe Persistent):
- Medium-dose ICS + LABA
- Consider tiotropium

Step 5 (Severe Persistent):
- High-dose ICS + LABA
- Consider omalizumab, mepolizumab, or other biologics

Step 6 (Severe Asthma):
- High-dose ICS + LABA + oral corticosteroid
- Biologics for appropriate phenotypes

Controller Medications:

Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS):
- First-line controller therapy
- Reduce inflammation and exacerbations
- Examples: Beclomethasone, budesonide, fluticasone

Long-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (LABA):
- Always used with ICS
- Examples: Salmeterol, formoterol
- Never as monotherapy

Combination Inhalers:
- ICS + LABA combinations
- Improve adherence
- Examples: Fluticasone/salmeterol, budesonide/formoterol

Leukotriene Modifiers:
- Montelukast, zafirlukast, zileuton
- Alternative to low-dose ICS
- Particularly useful in aspirin-sensitive asthma

Long-Acting Muscarinic Antagonist:
- Tiotropium (18 mcg daily)
- Add-on therapy for poorly controlled asthma

Biologics (for Severe Asthma):
1. Omalizumab (Anti-IgE):
   - Allergic asthma
   - IgE 30-700 IU/mL
   - Subcutaneous injection

2. Mepolizumab (Anti-IL-5):
   - Eosinophilic asthma
   - Blood eosinophils ≥150 cells/μL

3. Reslizumab (Anti-IL-5):
   - Eosinophilic asthma
   - Blood eosinophils ≥400 cells/μL

4. Benralizumab (Anti-IL-5 receptor):
   - Eosinophilic asthma
   - Blood eosinophils ≥300 cells/μL

5. Dupilumab (Anti-IL-4/IL-13):
   - Type 2 inflammation
   - Eosinophils ≥150 cells/μL or FeNO ≥25 ppb

Reliever Medications:

Short-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (SABA):
- Albuterol, levalbuterol
- First-line rescue medication
- Onset within minutes

Short-Acting Anticholinergics:
- Ipratropium bromide
- Adjunct to SABA in emergency settings

Oral Corticosteroids:
- Prednisone, prednisolone
- Severe exacerbations
- Short courses to minimize side effects

EXACERBATION MANAGEMENT

Mild-Moderate Exacerbation:
- Increased SABA use
- Peak flow 50-79% of personal best
- May manage at home

Treatment:
- SABA 2-4 puffs every 20 minutes × 3
- Oral corticosteroids if no improvement
- Follow up within 24-48 hours

Severe Exacerbation:
- Peak flow <50% of personal best
- Severe symptoms at rest
- Unable to speak in sentences

Emergency Management:
- High-flow oxygen
- Nebulized SABA + ipratropium
- Systemic corticosteroids
- Consider magnesium sulfate

Life-Threatening Asthma:
- Silent chest
- Cyanosis
- Bradycardia
- Hypotension
- Exhaustion

Treatment:
- Immediate intubation may be needed
- IV bronchodilators
- High-dose corticosteroids
- Consider heliox, ECMO

NON-PHARMACOLOGICAL MANAGEMENT

Environmental Control:
1. Allergen Avoidance:
   - House dust mite control
   - Pet allergen reduction
   - Mold prevention
   - Pollen avoidance

2. Irritant Avoidance:
   - Tobacco smoke cessation/avoidance
   - Air pollution reduction
   - Strong odors/chemicals
   - Cold air protection

Immunotherapy:
- Allergen-specific immunotherapy
- Subcutaneous or sublingual
- Consider for specific allergen sensitivity

Lifestyle Modifications:
- Weight management
- Regular exercise
- Stress reduction
- Adequate sleep

Education and Self-Management:
- Written asthma action plan
- Proper inhaler technique
- Peak flow monitoring
- Trigger identification and avoidance

SPECIAL POPULATIONS

Exercise-Induced Bronchoconstriction:
- Pre-exercise SABA
- Warm-up exercises
- Avoid cold, dry air
- Consider leukotriene modifiers

Pregnancy:
- Maintain good asthma control
- Most medications safe
- Avoid oral corticosteroids if possible
- Monitor closely

Elderly:
- Consider comorbidities
- Careful assessment of inhaler technique
- Drug interactions
- COPD overlap syndrome

Occupational Asthma:
- Remove from exposure if possible
- Workers' compensation considerations
- May improve with exposure cessation

MONITORING AND FOLLOW-UP

Regular Assessments:
- Symptom control
- Lung function (spirometry)
- Adherence to medications
- Inhaler technique
- Environmental exposures

Frequency:
- Every 1-6 months depending on control
- More frequent during step-up
- After exacerbations

Annual Review:
- Complete assessment of control
- Review of medications
- Inhaler technique check
- Written action plan update
- Allergy testing if indicated

PROGNOSIS

Factors Affecting Prognosis:
- Age of onset
- Severity at presentation
- Response to treatment
- Adherence to therapy
- Environmental exposures
- Comorbidities

Outcomes:
- Most children have improvement with age
- Adult-onset asthma often persists
- Good control achievable in majority
- Small subset develops severe disease

Complications:
- Respiratory failure
- Pneumothorax (rare)
- Atelectasis
- Chronic airflow limitation
- Side effects of medications

The management of asthma requires a comprehensive approach combining appropriate pharmacotherapy, environmental control measures, patient education, and regular monitoring to achieve optimal control and prevent exacerbations.
