Alzheimer's Disease

Overview:
Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that causes dementia, characterized by memory loss, cognitive decline, and behavioral changes. It is the most common cause of dementia in older adults.

Pathophysiology:
- Accumulation of amyloid-beta plaques in brain tissue
- Formation of neurofibrillary tangles containing hyperphosphorylated tau protein
- Progressive neuronal death and brain atrophy
- Cholinergic neurotransmitter deficiency
- Inflammation and oxidative stress

Clinical Presentation:
Early Stage:
- Mild memory loss, especially recent events
- Difficulty finding words
- Challenges with planning and organizing
- Mild disorientation in unfamiliar settings
- Changes in mood and personality

Middle Stage:
- Increased memory loss and confusion
- Difficulty recognizing family and friends
- Problems with language and reading
- Inability to learn new things
- Difficulty carrying out multistep tasks
- Problems coping with new situations
- Hallucinations, delusions, and paranoia
- Impulsive behavior

Late Stage:
- Severe memory loss
- Complete dependence on others
- Loss of ability to communicate
- Weight loss
- Seizures
- Difficulty swallowing
- Increased sleeping

Risk Factors:
- Age (greatest risk factor)
- Family history and genetics (APOE-e4 allele)
- History of head trauma
- Cardiovascular disease
- Diabetes
- Obesity
- Smoking
- Sleep disorders
- Social isolation

Diagnosis:
Clinical Assessment:
- Comprehensive medical history
- Mental status examinations (MMSE, MoCA)
- Neuropsychological testing
- Physical and neurological examination

Imaging:
- MRI to assess brain atrophy
- PET scans (amyloid, tau, FDG)
- Rule out other causes of dementia

Laboratory Tests:
- Complete blood count
- Comprehensive metabolic panel
- Thyroid function tests
- Vitamin B12 levels
- CSF biomarkers (research/specialized centers)

Differential Diagnosis:
- Vascular dementia
- Lewy body dementia
- Frontotemporal dementia
- Normal pressure hydrocephalus
- Depression ("pseudodementia")
- Medication-induced cognitive impairment
- Thyroid disorders
- Vitamin deficiencies

Treatment:
Pharmacological:
- Cholinesterase inhibitors: donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine
- NMDA receptor antagonist: memantine
- Aducanumab (controversial, limited evidence)
- Treatment of comorbid conditions

Non-pharmacological:
- Cognitive stimulation activities
- Regular physical exercise
- Social engagement
- Structured daily routines
- Environmental modifications
- Music and art therapy
- Reality orientation therapy

Behavioral Management:
- Address underlying causes of agitation
- Maintain consistent caregivers
- Modify environment to reduce confusion
- Use redirection techniques
- Consider low-dose antipsychotics for severe behavioral symptoms

Prognosis:
- Progressive disease with average survival 4-8 years after diagnosis
- Rate of progression varies among individuals
- Earlier onset typically associated with faster decline
- Quality of life depends on available support and care

Prevention:
- Regular physical exercise
- Mediterranean diet
- Cognitive engagement
- Social connections
- Cardiovascular health management
- Quality sleep
- Stress management

Complications:
- Falls and injuries
- Malnutrition and dehydration
- Infections (pneumonia, UTIs)
- Medication errors
- Wandering and getting lost
- Caregiver stress and burnout

Key Points:
- Most common cause of dementia
- Diagnosis is primarily clinical
- Early intervention may slow progression
- Focus on maintaining function and quality of life
- Comprehensive care approach involving multiple disciplines
- Important to provide support for caregivers
